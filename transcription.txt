The John G. Kerr Refuge for Insane
Fong Tsuen, Canton, China

Founded in 1898 by John G. Kerr, M. D., L. L. D., for forty-
seven years medical missionary to the Chinese in Canton
under the American Presbyterian Board, North.

Present Staff:

CHAS. C. SELDEN, Ph.D., M.D.,
Superintendent and Physician-in-charge.

J. ALLEN HOFMANN, Ph.B., M.D.,
Foreign Assistant Superintendent.

LOK LUN TENG, M.D.,
Chinese Assistant Superintendent.

PRINCIPLES UNDERLYING TREATMENT

1. These people are ill. If they act and speak unreas on
ably, it is not their fault.

2. This is a hospital, not a prison.

3. Though insane, these patients are yet men and women
—not beasts.

TREATMENT

1. The power of persuasion with—in the necessary cases
—the minimum of force.

2. Freedom with—in the few cases necessary—the min-
imum of restraint.

3. Out-of-door life, exercise, employment; rest, warm
baths; kindness mingled with firmness with—in the necessary
cases—the minimum of medicine.

[footnote] However, in No. 3, "the necessary cases" means nearly every case, for the
reason that the Chinese have unbounded confidence in the use of drugs, and
the friends of patients would not be at all satisfied if no medicine were given.

REPORT
1907 and 1908

(The last report was issued in 1904, just before the Superintendent
left for his furlough. He returned near the end of 1906.)

For 1907 there are a few important events to record in
the matters of the hospital. Dr. H. W. Boyd, who had
had charge during the Superintendent's absence in Amer-
ica, was reappointed by the Presbyterian Board to its South
China Mission.

Diphtheria, rather uncommon in South China, appeared
in the family of the native assistant physician who was
residing just outside the door of the hospital. Happily a
small supply of antitoxin was obtainable through the kind-
ness of Dr. Swan and Dr. Davenport and thus, by the good
hand of our Lord, the two stricken children were saved and
an epidemic among the insane patients was averted.

The great Missionary Conference in Shanghai in May
afforded an opportunity for speaking a few words about this
hospital and the need of establishing similar institutions
in other parts of China. Soon after his return from Shang-
hai the Superintendent was taken ill and was obliged for
six months to give up all work. During that time Rev.
A. Alf very kindly and very satisfactorily conducted the
business while the medical work was as ably looked after
by the native assistant and, for a short time also, Dr. Paul
J. Todd.

In the latter part of the year we were able to open a fine,
new building accommodating sixty-four patients. It is
built mostly of brick and reinforced concrete. It was a

great relief to be able to occupy this building as the others
were overcrowded. It was built with gifts from foreign
friends with the exception of a few hundred dollars given
by Chinese.

An epidemic of beri-beri also occurred in the hospital.
There were ten cases of which one, complicated by other
serious chronic trouble, died. During these last few years
beri-beri has become endemic in this region of China. In
1908 there was a very serious outbreak of the acute form in
the soldiers' barracks in Canton.

The year 1908 was full of important events touching the
hospital. In the beginning of the year a large band of
armed robbers broke a hole through the wall of the
grounds, crossed and making another hole on the opposite
side, entered a Chinese shop. Having procured several
hundred dollars worth of booty they returned by the same
route but forgot to fill up the holes they had made in the
walls.

That was coming rather near home. But in February
another small band of five armed robbers broke a hole
through one of the walls and entered the house just out-
side the door of the hospital occupied by the native assist-
ant physician and family and two Bible women. They
took away nearly every thing of any value after tying the
physician and his wife to the banisters.

In April we were rejoiced and relieved by the arrival of
a foreign assistant in the person of J. Allen Hofmann,
Ph.B., M.D., from America. We had been praying that
the Master would send the man whom He Himself had
chosen. Our association with Dr. Hofmann has been most
pleasant and he has proven himself to be a true yoke-
fellow. He was accompanied by his foster-mother, Mrs.
Olive A. Allen, who had for many years looked forward to
coming to China as a missionary.

In June Canton was visited by an epidemic of Asiatic
cholera. It reached the hospital just at the beginning of
the great flood which did such immense damage in this
province. Three smaller buildings were so flooded as re-
gards the ground floors that they could not be used, and
the patients had to be removed to other wards. This
necessitated crowding together without adequate means of
isolating those who were ill. The grounds were flooded
nearly every where with the infected water. Many patients
died. One woman attendant who had but five days before
begun work, died also. Many others of the inmates con-
tracted the disease but recovered. In order to lessen the
number of patients, all who had sufficiently recovered
mentally were hurried away, and some others not yet re-
covered were removed voluntarily by their families to get
them out of danger. So that the number of inmates
decreased in a short time by thirty. The flood lasted one
week only but it was followed after some days by a second
as high as the first, lasting, however, but one night.

In July occurred a typhoon, the greatest in the history
of Canton in forty years. Much damage was done on the
river as every where on the land. The losses to the hospital
amounted in repairs to over five hundred dollars, silver.
More than one-half of this was raised by subscription
among the Chinese physicians who had once been Dr.
Kerr's students. The superintendent's premises suffered
also to the extent of two hundred and thirty dollars.

In the autumn an epidemic of dysentery occurred which
again carried off a number of patients. We had also an
unusual number of cases of malarial fever. It was every-
where very prevalent.

During these two years the number of patients, notwith-
standing the sudden decrease at the time of the cholera
epidemic as noted above, has increased from 102 to 174. Of
the present number, 96 were committed to the hospital and

are supported by the officials. Of these 96, about one-half
were brought up from Hongkong and one-half picked up on
the streets of Canton by the police. Hongkong being a
British colony, those Chinese who have come from the
mainland and have become insane while in the colony are
sent to Canton and delivered over to a Chinese official.
That official then sends them to this hospital for treatment
instead of, as formerly, putting the serious cases in prison
and setting the harmless ones free in the street. Nearly
all, of whatever source, are brought to us in chains. These
are promptly removed on entering, as all chains were two
years ago relegated to the archeological museum.

The hospital continues to be, as from the beginning, in-
dependent of any board or society; but it is, nevertheless,
most distinctly a Christian missionary institution. While
its principal aim must ever be to witness as an object lesson,
in showing the Chinese what Christ's love will do for men,
we can see also that evangelistic work among the inmates
is not without fruit. Morning prayers are held each morn-
ing in the chapel attended by one-half the helpers and by
many of the patients who are able to come. A few verses
are read from the Gospels or from the Acts and explained.
Then there is singing accompanied by the organ, and
prayer, the whole occupying thirty minutes. We have a
prayer-meeting for the women employees attended also by
some of the women patients Tuesday afternoons of each
week, a class for Bible study for the attendants Wednesday
evening, a singing service Thursday evening; Sabbath
morning a Sabbath School for primary children gathered
from the neighborhood, a large number being boat children;
a Bible class for attendants and patients and later a
preaching service. The chapel doors are during this service
thrown open to the street for any passers-by who may desire
to come in and join us. The preachers for this service
are mainly students from the Presbyterian Theological

Seminary. There have been encouragements in this line.
Two of our former patients who recovered and became
Christians are valued assistants in other missions, one is
the wife of a native pastor and several others have received
baptism.

A dispensary for out-patients is open each week Monday
and Friday mornings, and the gospel message is given to
the men patients by our native evangelist, while the women
hear it through the resident Bible woman and the lady
missionary. Two of the women who have been listeners
more or less frequently for the past year have received
baptism.

With the exception of one Chinese Christian gentleman,
the Board of Trustees is made up of members of the
Presbyterian Mission. We appreciate highly the friendship
of the Presbyterian Board in New York, and wish to thank
that body for selecting and forwarding goods on various
occasions and especially for finding for us our valued
assistant Dr. Hofmann.

The daily running expenses are met by the income from
the patients, the room rent paid by the well-to-do helping to
make up for those who can pay nothing for their own support.
The foreign superintendents' salaries are special gifts.
Sufficient land for present use has already been procured
through gifts. But accommodations for housing the patients
are quite inadequate. Much of the time during these
two years a dozen to eighteen have been obliged to live in
a mat shed. One-half the male employees were also obliged
to live in a mat shed. Much of the land is low and must
be filled in; because in its present state it is a menace to
health and life. We need a wall about the newly acquired
property. We also very much need a new kitchen and
bath rooms as well as a house for the male attendants.
